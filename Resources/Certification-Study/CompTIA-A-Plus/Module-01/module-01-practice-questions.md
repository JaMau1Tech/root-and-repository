## 1.2 — The Troubleshooting Methodology

**Q1.** A user reports their monitor won't turn on. You've gathered information and confirmed the theory that the power cable is loose. What is the correct NEXT step?

A. Document the findings
B. Test the theory to determine the cause
C. Establish a plan of action and implement the solution
D. Verify full system functionality

**Correct: C — Establish a plan of action and implement the solution.**
Why: Step 2 (establish a theory) and step 3 (test the theory) are already done — the theory was "confirmed." The next step in the 6-step order is step 4: decide how to fix it (repair/replace/workaround) and actually do it.

- **A is wrong** — Documentation is step 6, the *last* step. Nothing has been fixed or verified yet.
- **B is wrong** — Testing the theory is step 3, and the question states the theory is already confirmed — testing it again is redundant and out of order.
- **D is wrong** — Verification (step 5) happens *after* a fix is implemented, not before.

---

**Q2.** A technician plugs the cable back in and the monitor works. What should they do before closing the ticket?

A. Immediately close the ticket — the problem is gone
B. Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures
C. Re-establish a new theory of probable cause
D. Escalate to a senior technician

**Correct: B — Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures.**
Why: Step 5 explicitly says don't close a ticket the moment the symptom disappears — confirm the fix holds and the system is fully functional end-to-end, and put a preventive measure in place (e.g., securing the cable) so it doesn't recur.

- **A is wrong** — This skips verification entirely, which is exactly the mistake step 5 exists to prevent.
- **C is wrong** — A new theory is only needed if the *first* theory was disproven. Here the fix already worked.
- **D is wrong** — Escalation happens when an issue is beyond your skill/knowledge/scope — not applicable to a fix that already worked.

---

**Q3.** You test your first theory (bad RAM) and it turns out the RAM is fine. What should you do?

A. Escalate immediately, since your theory was wrong
B. Document that the issue is unsolvable
C. Establish a new theory and test it
D. Re-test the same RAM theory a second time to be sure

**Correct: C — Establish a new theory and test it.**
Why: Step 3 explicitly states that a disproven theory is a normal, expected outcome, not a failure — form a new theory (step 2 again) and keep testing.

- **A is wrong** — One disproven theory isn't automatically grounds for escalation. Escalation is for when you're out of skill/knowledge/scope, not after a single wrong guess.
- **B is wrong** — Nothing in the methodology supports declaring an issue unsolvable after one failed theory.
- **D is wrong** — Re-testing the exact same theory that just failed wastes time and won't produce new information.

**Q4.** Why is "what changed recently?" considered one of the most useful questions in step 1 (Identify the Problem)?

A. It's required by CompTIA's exam objectives
B. Most problems trace back to something that changed, and users often won't volunteer that information unless asked directly
C. It replaces the need to reproduce the problem
D. It determines whether to escalate the ticket

**Correct: B.**
Why: This is stated directly in the notes — most problems trace back to something that changed, and it's information users tend not to offer unprompted, making it disproportionately high-value to ask.

- **A is wrong** — The troubleshooting methodology, including this question, is a *competency standard* per the objectives doc, not a numbered/scored exam objective itself.
- **C is wrong** — Reproducing the problem is a separate, later action (used in step 2/3 to test theories), not something "what changed" replaces.
- **D is wrong** — Escalation is governed by skill/knowledge/scope limits (step 4), unrelated to this specific question.

**Q5.** A technician determines a server is being decommissioned next month, so instead of replacing a failing RAID array, they implement a temporary restore. Is this an acceptable resolution?

A. No — every issue must be permanently resolved before closing a ticket
B. Yes — a workaround is acceptable when it's feasible and business priorities support it
C. No — RAID failures specifically always require hardware replacement
D. Yes, but only if a senior technician personally approves every workaround

**Correct: B.**
Why: Step 4 explicitly states not every issue needs to be permanently resolved on the spot — a workaround is fine when business priorities support it, using exactly this decommissioning scenario as the example.

- **A is wrong** — This directly contradicts step 4's workaround allowance.
- **C is wrong** — Nothing in the notes singles out RAID as an exception requiring permanent fixes; the "repair, replace, or workaround" decision applies generally.
- **D is wrong** — Nothing in the methodology requires senior sign-off on every workaround; that's an invented constraint not found in the notes.

## 1.1 — Skills and Technical Knowledge Areas

**Q6.** Which of the following are the five technical knowledge areas an IT specialist is expected to have broad working knowledge of?

A. Hardware, Software, Networking, Cloud, Security
B. Hardware, Software, Databases, Cloud, Management
C. Networking, Security, Programming, Cloud, Hardware
D. Hardware, Software, Networking, Cloud, Certifications

**Correct: A.**
Why: Stated directly in 1.1.2 — these five map roughly onto CompTIA's own domain structure (Mobile Devices, Networking, Hardware, Virtualization/Cloud, Troubleshooting) and preview the shape of the whole course.

- **B, C, D are wrong** — Each swaps in an area (Databases, Programming, Management, Certifications) that isn't one of the five named technical knowledge areas in the notes. Certifications are discussed separately (as validating these areas), not as one of the five areas itself.

---

**Q7.** What is the core identity of an IT specialist, according to Module 1?

A. Someone who has memorized the answer to every possible technical problem
B. A problem solver who applies a consistent, repeatable process
C. A specialist who only handles one specific technology area
D. Someone whose primary skill is fast typing and system navigation

**Correct: B.**
Why: Stated directly in 1.1.1 — the role's value isn't in already knowing every answer; it's in reliably applying a consistent process (the 6-step methodology) to find it, given the ever-changing nature of the job.

- **A is wrong** — Explicitly contradicted by the notes: "the job is really about having a repeatable process, not knowing every answer."
- **C is wrong** — The notes describe broad knowledge across five areas (hardware, software, networking, cloud, security), not narrow specialization.
- **D is wrong** — Not mentioned anywhere in the notes as a defining trait.
